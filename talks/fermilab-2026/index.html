Energy loss in a thin layer

PDG §34.2.9
A thin layer does not stop the muon. The deposit \(\Delta\) follows Landau's distribution: a peak at \(\Delta_p\) and a long tail from rare hard collisions.
\[ f(\Delta)=\frac{\varphi(\lambda)}{\xi},\quad \lambda=\frac{\Delta-\Delta_p}{\xi}-0.22278, \qquad \varphi(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-t\ln t-\lambda t}\sin(\pi t)\,dt \]
\[ \xi=\frac{K}{2}\Big\langle\frac{Z}{A}\Big\rangle\frac{x}{\beta^2},\qquad \Delta_p=\xi\Big[\ln\frac{2mc^2\beta^2\gamma^2}{I}+\ln\frac{\xi}{I}+0.200-\beta^2-\delta(\beta\gamma)\Big] \]
PDG, Passage of particles through matter, Eq. 34.12. L. Landau, J. Phys. USSR 8 (1944) 201.
A muon at minimum ionization (274 MeV, \(\beta\gamma=3.45\)) in 300 µm Si: \(\xi=5.80\) keV, \(\Delta_p=78.6\) keV.

def landau(l):       # Landau's phi; scipy.stats.landau is the S1 form, shifted and scaled
    return 2 / np.pi * scipy.stats.landau.pdf(2 / np.pi * (l - np.log(np.pi / 2)))

T   = 10 ** rng.uniform(np.log10(300), 5, n)         # 0.3-100 GeV [MeV]
cos = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.0, n)                       # zenith angle up to 60 deg
xi, dp = landau_params(T, 300e-4 * 2.329 / cos)      # PDG Eq. 34.12, 300 um of Si
q = np.linspace(0, 0.4, 401)                         # deposited energy [MeV]
for c in range(0, n, 1000):                          # 1000 muons x 401 bins per call
    x, d = xi[c:c + 1000, None], dp[c:c + 1000, None]
    l = (q - d) / x - 0.22278

The recipe

  1. Approximate the expensive \(f\) with polynomials
  2. Evaluate the polynomials instead, a handful of fused multiply-adds (we will come back to this)
  3. Pay once, at fit time
The cost per call becomes flat, independent of how expensive \(f\) was.

Horner and Clenshaw

one polynomial, two bases
degree 7 for fit<8>
monomials \(\sum c_k x^k\): Horner
Chebyshev \(\sum c_k T_k(x)\): Clenshaw, \(M=\begin{bmatrix}2x&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\), \(e=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}\)
Both run from the innermost bracket out. Clenshaw is Horner with the \(2\times2\) matrix \(M\) in place of \(x\): from \(T_{k+1}=2xT_k-T_{k-1}\), \((b_k,b_{k+1})=c_k\,e+M\,(b_{k+1},b_{k+2})\). Horner: one fused multiply-add per degree. Clenshaw: one fused multiply-add and one subtraction per degree.

The degree is set by \(f\)

Bernstein ellipse
If \(f\) is analytic inside the ellipse \(E_\rho\) (foci \(\pm1\)), the error on Chebyshev nodes falls like \(\rho^{-n}\). Landau on \([-5,\,60]\): \(\rho=\) 1.17 per degree.
The convergence depends only on the nodes, not on the basis: we can fit monomials on Chebyshev nodes and achieve the same convergence.
L. N. Trefethen, Approximation Theory and Approximation Practice, Thm 8.2; Six myths of polynomial interpolation and quadrature, Math. Today 47, 2011, Myth 1. After A. Barnett, FWAM 2025, slide 11.

Monomial fit

shift and scale to \([-1,1]\)
\[ t=\frac{2x-(a+b)}{b-a}\in[-1,1], \qquad p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d} c_k\, t^{k}, \qquad p(x_j)=f_j \]
\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t_0 & \cdots & t_0^{d}\\ 1 & t_1 & \cdots & t_1^{d}\\ \vdots & & & \vdots\\ 1 & t_d & \cdots & t_d^{d}\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c_0\\ c_1\\ \vdots\\ c_{d}\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} f_0\\ f_1\\ \vdots\\ f_d\end{bmatrix} \]
Sample \(f\) at the \(d+1\) Chebyshev nodes, solve one Vandermonde system, keep the coefficients.
Shift and scale first. \(t=(x-27.5)/32.5\) maps \([-5,\,60]\) to \([-1,1]\). On \([-5,\,60]\), \(x^k\) reaches \(60^k\); on \([-1,1]\), \(|t^k|\le1\).

How a panel is sampled

treeweave's rule
  • Fit. \(f\) at the 8 nodes \(t_k=\cos\frac{(2k+1)\pi}{16}\), Chebyshev of the first kind. 8 monomial coefficients in \(t\), the panel mapped to \([-1,1]\).
  • Check. \(|f-p|\) at the 8 cell midpoints \(t=\pm\frac18,\pm\frac38,\pm\frac58, \pm\frac78\), against the tolerance.
  • Fail. Bisect. Each half calls \(f\) 16 more times.

Low order. Many panels. Vector-friendly.

SIMD: a vector register holds 4 (AVX2) or 8 (AVX-512) doubles, one per lane. One instruction acts on every lane.
A degree-7 Horner over eight lanes is the same instruction count whatever \(f\) was.
  • Locate the panel — a table lookup, or a descent through the tree
  • Horner on its 8 stored coefficients — 7 fused multiply-adds
  • Nothing else. No branches, no libm, no memory beyond the coefficient table

The treeweave alternative

one fit, no log at eval
g4bench.cppc++
double plog2(double t) {  // T = 2^e m  ->  e + (m - 1)
  auto b = std::bit_cast<uint64_t>(t);
  return int(b >> 52) - 1023 + std::bit_cast<double>(b & 0xFFFFFFFFFFFFF | 0x3FF0000000000000) - 1; }
double pexp2(double v) {  // the inverse: e + r  ->  2^e (1 + r)
  double e = std::floor(v); return std::ldexp(1 + (v - e), int(e)); }
auto f = treeweave::fit<4>([](double v) { return bethe(pexp2(v)); }, 0.0, 32.0, 2.4e-4);
double dedx(double T) { return f(plog2(T)); }   // float: 32-bit masks, float fit
plog2 reads the exponent and the mantissa bits: \(T=2^e m\to e+(m-1)\). It is exact and monotone, and pexp2 inverts it. bethe(pexp2(v)) is smooth between integers, so fit<4> on \([0,32)\) fits it with 40 leaves.

A more general function: the Hankel function

Hankel functions of the first kind, \(H_0^{(1)}(x)\) and \(H_1^{(1)}(x)\), for all \(x>0\).
\[ H_n^{(1)}(x) = J_n(x) + i\,Y_n(x) \]
The 2-D Helmholtz Green's function. The kernel of every 2-D fast multipole method, every scattering solver, every boundary integral code in the plane.
The reference implementation is hank103.f — Rokhlin and Gimbutas, Fortran, hand-tuned over thirty years. It runs at 13.1 M evals/s on one core.

Three different reasons

Low \(x\) — a logarithmic branch point at the origin
\[ Y_0(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\Big(\ln\tfrac{x}{2}+\gamma\Big)J_0(x) + \frac{2}{\pi} \sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}H_k}{(k!)^2}\Big(\tfrac{x}{2}\Big)^{2k} \]
DLMF 10.8.2 · no polynomial of any degree approximates \(\ln x\) near 0 to a relative tolerance
Mid \(x\) — 63 zeros of \(J_0\) and \(Y_0\) in \([1,101)\)
A pointwise relative tolerance can never be met at a zero of the target.
High \(x\) — an unbounded domain, \(x\ge101\)
\(|H_n^{(1)}(x)|\sim\sqrt{2/(\pi x)}\) oscillates forever: no finite set of panels covers \([101,\infty)\).

The cure is arithmetic, not a better fitter

Do the analysis first, and fit only what is left.
  1. Split the domain at the features
  2. Transform each regime so the remainder is polynomial-friendly
  3. Fit the remainder
  4. Add the analytic part back, possibly vectorizing the fixup
Nothing here is specific to Hankel functions.

The Lebesgue constant

why Chebyshev, 2 of 2
\[ p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_k\,x^k,\quad Vc=f,\quad V_{jk}=x_j^k, \qquad \ell_j(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}(V^{-1})_{kj}\,x^k \] \[ \Lambda_d=\max_{x\in[-1,1]}\sum_{j=0}^{d}|\ell_j(x)|, \qquad \|f-p\|_\infty\le(1+\Lambda_d)\,\|f-p^*\|_\infty \]
The comparison is against \(p^*\), the best polynomial of degree \(d\). Interpolation is linear and reproduces \(p^*\), so \(p-p^*\) interpolates \(f-p^*\) and \(\|p-p^*\|_\infty\le\Lambda_d\|f-p^*\|_\infty\): \(\Lambda_d\) is the \(\infty\)-norm of the interpolation operator. For fit<8> (degree 7, first-kind nodes) \(\Lambda=2.3\): at most \(3.3\times\) worse. The plot uses extrema nodes: 2.20 at \(d=7\). In double, the monomial \(\Lambda_d\) breaks once \(\mathrm{cond}(V)\) nears \(1/\varepsilon\).
